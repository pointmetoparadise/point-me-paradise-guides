Bergamo, Two Cities on a Hill

A weekend of walled streets, long dinners, and a funicular ride between two very different versions of the same city.

Bergamo splits neatly into two moods, and you're going to spend your trip moving between them. Up on the hill, medieval streets, church interiors that don't match their plain facades, and a bell that still rings out a curfew nobody has to obey anymore. Down below, a modern Italian city with proper shopping streets and its own restaurants worth sitting down for. You don't have to choose — the funicular takes four minutes, and this guide is built to get you back and forth without wasting a single one of them.

Orientation

The City in Three Layers

Understanding the geography here is half the fun of visiting it.

Think of Bergamo as three stacked levels. Città Bassa is the lower, modern city — where you'll likely arrive and where a lot of everyday Bergamo actually happens. Città Alta is the walled hilltop town and the reason you booked this trip: medieval lanes, Renaissance squares, churches stacked with more art than their exteriors let on. Above that again is San Vigilio, a quieter hill village with its own funicular and its own view.

Why this layout works for you

You can start the morning somewhere contemporary, ride a 19th-century funicular into a walled medieval town by midday, and end the evening looking out over green hills toward the Alps — all without a car, a transfer, or much planning.

Getting Around

Upper Town, Lower Town, and the Funicular Between Them

The ride itself is part of the sightseeing, not just a way to skip a hill.

The historic funicular connecting Città Bassa and Città Alta has been running since 1887, and it drops you right at Piazza Mercato delle Scarpe, in the heart of the old town. A second, separate funicular climbs from Città Alta up to San Vigilio, for when you want to go one level higher.

Google Maps — Città Alta Funicular

Take one direction by funicular and walk the other. The old stairways and gates between the two towns are worth experiencing on foot at least once, and it's a nicer way to arrive than stepping straight off a platform.

Città Alta

The Walled Town, Beyond the Main Street

The main street is the introduction. The side lanes are the actual city.

Città Alta sits across seven hills inside the Venetian Walls, and it's compact enough to feel entirely walkable in a day — medieval streets, Renaissance buildings, small cafés and shops tucked between them. The main pedestrian spine, known locally as the Corsarola, runs along Via Gombito and Via Colleoni. It's the street everyone photographs, and it's genuinely pretty, but don't let it be the whole visit.

Google Maps — Città Alta

Turn off onto Via Arena, Via Salvecchio, Via San Lorenzo, or Via Porta Dipinta. These are the quieter streets where Città Alta stops feeling like a stage set and starts feeling like a neighborhood.

The Historic Heart

Piazza Vecchia

Every version of Bergamo's history is standing around the edges of one square.

Piazza Vecchia is where Bergamo's history collects in one place: Palazzo della Ragione, Palazzo Nuovo, Palazzo del Podestà, the Campanone tower, and the Contarini Fountain all face into the same square. Palazzo della Ragione dates back to the late 1100s and is considered the oldest surviving municipal seat in Lombardy — it doesn't look like a museum piece, which is part of what makes it work.

Google Maps — Piazza Vecchia

Look underneath Palazzo della Ragione for the 18th-century sundial set into the pavement. At solar noon, light passes through an opening above and marks the date and zodiac sign. Go early for photos with no one in frame, and come back in the evening for the atmosphere — it's a different square after dark.

A Bergamo Tradition

The Campanone, and What Happens at 10 PM

This is the one thing worth rearranging your evening around.

Plan to be in Città Alta at 10 PM

Every evening, the Campanone — the civic tower dominating Piazza Vecchia — rings 100 times, a tradition dating back to when the city gates closed for the night. If you're staying over, find a quiet spot somewhere near the square and just listen. It's not a photo. It's the kind of thing you remember instead of the thing you photographed.

Beyond the evening ritual, the Campanone also gives you one of the city's best elevated views if you go up during the day.

Google Maps — Campanone
Church-Hopping, Reconsidered

Piazza Duomo and Its Three Churches

A tiny square holding one of the highest concentrations of important architecture in the city.

Walk through the arches beneath Palazzo della Ragione and you land, almost without warning, in tiny Piazza Duomo — Bergamo Cathedral, the Basilica di Santa Maria Maggiore, the Cappella Colleoni, and the Baptistery all within a few steps of each other.

Google Maps — Piazza Duomo

Basilica di Santa Maria Maggiore

Even if you feel done with Italian churches by this point in the trip, go inside this one. The exterior is fairly restrained and gives you no warning for what's waiting inside — tapestries, gilding, frescoes, carved wood, a genuinely rich Baroque interior. Composer Gaetano Donizetti is buried here. Visiting it right alongside the Cappella Colleoni next door makes the contrast between the two even more striking.

Google Maps — Santa Maria Maggiore

Cappella Colleoni

The marble facade sets this chapel apart from everything around it immediately. It was built as the mausoleum of Bartolomeo Colleoni, the powerful 15th-century military commander tied to the Venetian Republic.

Google Maps — Cappella Colleoni

Find the Colleoni coat of arms near the entrance and rub the three polished symbols — it's a longstanding visitor tradition, and Bergamo's own tourism authority lists it among the city's essential local experiences.

Evening Strolls

The Venetian Walls

Bergamo's biggest monument doubles as its evening promenade.

The roughly six-kilometer wall system was built starting in 1561 under the Republic of Venice and is now part of a UNESCO World Heritage site — but in practice, it's just where Bergamo goes to unwind. Locals walk dogs, jog, and sit along the walls as the sun goes down, and there's no reason you shouldn't join them.

Google Maps — Venetian Walls

Time your walk for the final hour before sunset. It's the best hour on the walls, and it's free.

Porta San Giacomo

This is probably Bergamo's most photogenic gate — white Zandobbio marble, still carrying the Lion of Saint Mark from Venetian rule, and once the grand entrance for travelers arriving from Milan. The approach across the stone bridge is lovely on its own, but the views over Città Bassa from here are even better. Come around sunset if you can.

Google Maps — Porta San Giacomo
A Quieter View

The Rocca

Same view, considerably fewer people.

The Rocca sits on the hill of Sant'Eufemia on the east side of Città Alta, and the surrounding Parco delle Rimembranze gives you a wide, elevated view over the old city, the lower city, and the countryside beyond. It's one of the better places to step away from the crowds around Piazza Vecchia without giving up the view.

Google Maps — Rocca di Bergamo
One Hill Further

San Vigilio

Take the second funicular and the city changes character again.

San Vigilio sits above Città Alta and feels almost like a separate hill village — the second funicular runs from near Porta Sant'Alessandro straight up to it. Once there, you'll find quiet lanes, restaurants, gardens, and the ruins of Castello di San Vigilio. Late afternoon is the best time to be up here, followed by dinner either at the top or back down in Città Alta.

Google Maps — San Vigilio Google Maps — Castello di San Vigilio

Worth the extra funicular ride

The views alone justify the trip up — greener, quieter, and more panoramic than anything in Città Alta itself.

Food & Drink

What to Eat in Bergamo

This is mountain food dressed up in an Italian city, and it's worth taking seriously.

Bergamo's food is richer and more mountain-influenced than the Italian cooking most visitors expect — butter, cheese, stuffed pasta, polenta, slow-cooked meats. It's recognized by UNESCO's Creative Cities network for gastronomy, with a dairy heritage that includes nine PDO cheeses.

Casoncelli alla Bergamasca

If you only eat one dish here, make it this. These stuffed pasta parcels come dressed with browned butter, sage, pancetta, and cheese — Bergamo's tourism authority calls it one of the gastronomic symbols of the territory, and it isn't overselling it.

Polenta Taragna

Polenta with cheese worked into it, rich and creamy, usually served alongside braised beef, rabbit, or roast meats. This is Bergamo comfort food in its purest form.

Stracciatella Gelato

Order this in the name of historical research — stracciatella was invented right here in Bergamo in 1961, by Enrico Panattoni at La Marianna, when hot chocolate poured into cold fiordilatte gelato created the irregular chocolate shards that give it its name. La Marianna still operates today at Colle Aperto.

Google Maps — La Marianna

Local Cheese

Look for Taleggio DOP, Strachitunt DOP, Formai de Mut, Branzi, and Agrì di Valtorta. None of these are tourist add-ons — dairy and mountain cheese production are genuinely central to Bergamo's food identity.

The yellow "polenta" that isn't polenta

The bright yellow cakes you'll see in pastry shop windows, shaped like little loaves of polenta, are actually dessert — sponge cake, cream, and marzipan under a bit of chocolate decoration. Buy one from a traditional pasticceria as a snack or a souvenir that doesn't need refrigeration.

Shopping

Where to Shop, Upper Town and Lower Town

Two very different shopping streets, both worth an evening stroll.

Down in Città Bassa, Via XX Settembre is Bergamo's main modern pedestrian shopping street — Italian and international brands, cafés, and independent shops woven in between. It's a good spot for an evening stroll once dinner reservations are behind you.

Google Maps — Via XX Settembre

Up in Città Alta, Via Colleoni and the lanes around it hold small specialty shops worth ten minutes each — local cheese, Valcalepio wine, polenta products, regional sweets, leather goods, and Italian stationery all make sensible souvenirs that survive the flight home.

Città Bassa deserves a slot in your itinerary

It's easy to let Città Alta's history overshadow the lower city, but Città Bassa is contemporary Bergamo — Porta Nuova, Sentierone, and Piazza Pontida give you a genuinely different read on the place, with broad avenues and Art Nouveau architecture standing in contrast to the medieval streets above.

Beyond the Corsarola

Borgo Pignolo and the Quieter Streets

The connector street between the two towns is worth a slow walk on its own.

Borgo Pignolo links the upper and lower parts of the city and makes for one of Bergamo's nicest walking corridors — historic palazzi, small churches, little shops, and residential streets, with Accademia Carrara and GAMeC both nearby if you want to build a longer afternoon around it.

Google Maps — Borgo Pignolo

If you're heading back down from Città Alta and want an alternative to the funicular, take Via Porta Dipinta instead. It leads toward Porta Sant'Agostino through one of the quieter, more attractive stretches of the old city, and pairs naturally with a stop at Palazzo Moroni along the way.

Google Maps — Via Porta Dipinta
If You Have More Time

Accademia Carrara and Palazzo Moroni

Two stops for the days when you want more than churches and squares.

Accademia Carrara is surprisingly overlooked by international visitors, given that its collection includes Raphael, Botticelli, Bellini, Mantegna, Titian, Lorenzo Lotto, and Giovanni Battista Moroni. For anyone with a genuine interest in Italian Renaissance painting, it's one of the most important stops in the city.

Google Maps — Accademia Carrara

Palazzo Moroni is a historic residence with frescoed rooms and period furnishings, but don't stop inside — the property opens into formal Italian gardens, terraces, and orchards, a very different pace from the churches and public squares that fill most of a Città Alta itinerary.

Google Maps — Palazzo Moroni
Practical Details

Planning Your Days

One night changes the whole trip — here's why.

How much time to give it

A half day covers Piazza Vecchia, the churches, and a walk along the walls. A full day gives you a genuine introduction. Two days is comfortable for a first visit, and a third day lets you get into the food, the art, and the surrounding hills. If your schedule allows even one overnight, take it — Bergamo after the day visitors leave is a different city.

Best time to visit: April–May brings mild weather and comfortable walking temperatures. June adds long evenings and easy outdoor dining. July–August is warmest and busiest — Bergamo doesn't get Florence or Venice-level crowds, but the hills can be tiring at midday heat. September–October may be the best overall combination: cooler air, green hills, and the kind of hearty Bergamasque food that suddenly makes complete sense. November through February is quieter and more atmospheric, with fog and gray days possible, though Città Alta still holds its own in winter.

Worth Knowing Before You Go

A Few Things to Skip

Don't rush it

Don't treat Bergamo as a two-hour stop on the way to somewhere else, and don't assume seeing Piazza Vecchia means you've seen the city. Stay off Via Colleoni long enough to find the side streets, and don't default to the first restaurant facing the square just because the tables look nice — better meals are a few streets over.

Don't skip Città Bassa on the assumption that Città Alta is the whole story — the upper town shows you Bergamo's history, the lower town shows you Bergamo today. Wear proper walking shoes: cobblestones, hills, stairs, and polished old paving stones are not a forgiving combination. And if your schedule allows it, don't leave before sunset — the walls are one of the best parts of the trip, and they're free.

Florence, In a Day

David in the morning, wine windows by afternoon, the skyline at the end of it — built for the one day you've got.

Florence rewards people who plan the big reservations ahead and then leave room to wander — and with one day, that planning matters even more. This is the version of the city built to fit: the sight worth booking first thing, the walk that gives you the postcard shots without wasted steps, the neighborhood that feels like Florence rather than a queue, and a clear line under what to leave for next time.

How to Spend Your One Day

You get one shot at this city today, so the order matters more than the list.

Start at the Accademia before the tour groups build up, then work your way through the Duomo area and down to the river. Cross for lunch and a slow afternoon in the Oltrarno, then climb back up in the last hour of daylight for the view. That's a full day of walking — comfortable shoes, not sandals.

The One Rule for Today

Leave the Uffizi and the Pitti Palace for a return trip. The Uffizi alone deserves two to three hours, and trying to add it — plus the Accademia, the Duomo, and Boboli — to one day means you'll remember security lines more clearly than the art. Pick fewer things and actually see them.

Start With David

See him before the tour groups do.

Galleria dell'Accademia

This is where the original David lives, and it's more impressive in person than any photo suggests. Michelangelo's unfinished Prisoners line the hallway leading up to it, and seeing the rough-cut marble figures right before the finished one changes how you look at it.

Open in Google Maps: Accademia Gallery

Reserve ahead — this one gets crowded fast in peak season. Current hours run 8:15 AM–6:50 PM, and the museum is normally closed Mondays. Give it 60–90 minutes.

The Duomo, From Outside

The dome is worth seeing whether or not you climb it.

The cathedral complex is the visual center of Florence — the cathedral itself, Brunelleschi's Dome, Giotto's Bell Tower, the Baptistery, and the Opera del Duomo Museum all cluster around Piazza del Duomo. The cathedral is free to walk into. Climbing the dome means 463 steps, no elevator, and a fixed reservation time that isn't changeable or refundable, so with one day it's reasonable to skip the climb and get your rooftop view later at Piazzale Michelangelo instead. One current note: the Baptistery's ceiling mosaics are under restoration and aren't visible right now.

Open in Google Maps: Duomo / Historic Center

Dress for the Churches

Shoulders and knees should be covered to enter — the Duomo specifically turns away bare shoulders and legs. Worth knowing before you plan an outfit for the whole day.

If You Want to Add One More Stop

Two ways to stretch the morning without wrecking the rest of the day.

Climb the Dome

If you're willing to start even earlier, Brunelleschi's Dome is bookable separately from the cathedral itself. It's 463 steps with no elevator, and the reservation locks you into a fixed time slot that isn't changeable or refundable — so this only works if you commit to it before anything else. The current Brunelleschi Pass runs €30, covers the Dome, Bell Tower, Baptistery, Opera del Duomo Museum, and the Santa Reparata excavations, and stays valid across three calendar days.

Book directly through the Opera del Duomo's own site — resale tickets bought through third parties can be turned away at the door.

Bargello National Museum

If sculpture interests you more than painting and you'd rather skip the Uffizi crowds altogether, the Bargello is the trade worth knowing about. The medieval building holds work by Donatello, Michelangelo, Verrocchio, Della Robbia, Giambologna, and Cellini, and draws a fraction of the Accademia's crowd. Rick Steves specifically calls it underappreciated.

Open in Google Maps: Bargello Museum

Piazza della Signoria to the River

This stretch is the postcard walk, and it earns it.

Piazza della Signoria & Palazzo Vecchio

The civic heart of historic Florence. A replica of David stands outside Palazzo Vecchio where the original once did, and the Loggia dei Lanzi beside it works as an open-air sculpture gallery — worth a slow lap even if you're skipping the museum.

Open in Google Maps: Piazza della Signoria

Ponte Vecchio

Yes, it's crowded, and yes, you should still walk it. The medieval bridge is lined with shops, mostly jewelry and gold now. Cross it, then double back to Ponte Santa Trinita for the actual photograph — that's the angle that gets Ponte Vecchio itself in the frame.

Open in Google Maps: Ponte Vecchio

The natural route is Duomo → Piazza della Signoria → Arno → Ponte Vecchio, all on foot, no backtracking.

Lunch, Before You Cross Back

Eat where the market crowd eats, not where someone's holding a photo menu outside.

Sant'Ambrogio Market or Mercato Centrale

Sant'Ambrogio is the covered market that's been running since 1873 and still feels like a neighborhood errand rather than a tourist stop — but it closes at 2 PM, Monday through Saturday, so time it. Mercato Centrale is the easier fallback: the ground floor is a traditional market, the upper floor is a food hall with multiple vendors that stays open until midnight.

Open in Google Maps: Sant'Ambrogio Market Open in Google Maps: Mercato Centrale

If you want to eat something distinctly Florentine, look for lampredotto — slow-cooked tripe served in a roll with salsa verde, sold at stalls around both markets. If that's not your speed, ribollita is the gentler option: a bread-and-vegetable soup built on beans and cavolo nero.

Across the River: Oltrarno

This is where Florence stops feeling like one enormous museum.

The Oltrarno has historically been the artisan side of the city, and Santo Spirito in particular is where the pace slows down — neighborhood piazzas, workshops, wine bars, and far fewer people holding phones over their heads.

Piazza Santo Spirito

A lively square that hosts markets and remains one of the more genuinely social spaces in Florence — worth sitting in for ten minutes rather than photographing and moving on.

Open in Google Maps: Piazza Santo Spirito

Piazza della Passera

A tiny square that's easy to walk past entirely between Ponte Vecchio and Santo Spirito — small enough that you could miss it if you're not looking, which is part of the appeal.

Open in Google Maps: Piazza della Passera

A Wine Window

One of Florence's better historical quirks: tiny openings built into palace walls so noble families could sell wine straight from their estates. About 170 survive across the city, and a handful — including Babae in Santo Spirito — are back in use.

Open in Google Maps: Babae Wine Window

If You're Staying for Dinner

Consider spending the evening around Santo Spirito instead of heading back toward the Duomo. Dinner also runs later here than you might expect — a table around 8:30–9 PM is closer to the local rhythm than an early American dinner hour.

What to Buy (and What to Skip)

The difference between a keepsake and a refrigerator magnet is which street you buy it on.

Oltrarno Artisan Workshops

The Santo Spirito–San Frediano stretch of the Oltrarno has long been Florence's artisan quarter — jewelry, silverwork, violin making, woodwork, and fabric painting, made by the people selling it.

Open in Google Maps: Oltrarno Artisan District

Officina Profumo-Farmaceutica di Santa Maria Novella

Part shop, part historical experience. The rooms are frescoed, the furnishings are original, and the pharmaceutical vessels on display go back centuries — a good stop for perfume, soap, or a gift that isn't a magnet.

Open in Google Maps: Santa Maria Novella Pharmacy

Skip This
San Lorenzo Leather Market

You'll see leather jackets, belts, and bags everywhere here, but Florence's own tourism authority describes this outdoor market as tourist-oriented — don't assume "sold in Florence" means "made in Florence."

Something Sweet

Good gelato doesn't need to look like a special effect.

One rule solves most of this: avoid the giant, unnaturally bright mountains piled high above the tubs — it's one of the most repeated warnings from people who actually live in Florence. Look instead for gelato kept flat or in covered metal containers, with colors that look like actual fruit and pistachio rather than a cartoon.

Open in Google Maps: Gelateria della Passera

Golden Hour: The View You Came For

Skip the crowd at the overlook and keep walking uphill.

Rose Garden

Free, quieter than the piazzale above it, and with genuinely good views over the city. It also holds sculptures by Belgian artist Jean-Michel Folon and a small Japanese garden, a gift from Florence's sister city, Kyoto.

Open in Google Maps: Rose Garden

Piazzale Michelangelo

This is the Florence postcard — the Duomo, Palazzo Vecchio, Santa Croce, Ponte Vecchio, and the Arno laid out in one panorama. Sunset here is the classic move, and the crowd is the tradeoff.

Open in Google Maps: Piazzale Michelangelo

San Miniato al Monte

A few extra minutes uphill from the piazzale, and the crowd thins out considerably. The church is one of the city's best examples of Tuscan Romanesque architecture, with an 11th-century crypt, medieval floor mosaics, and views that rival the more famous overlook below it. Certain Saturday evening vigils bring Gregorian chant into the crypt.

Open in Google Maps: San Miniato al Monte

The Order That Works

Rose Garden first, then Piazzale Michelangelo, then the extra climb to San Miniato as the light drops. Each stop gets quieter as the sun goes down and the crowd peels off after their photo at the piazzale.

Dinner, If You're Staying Into the Evening

You're already up the hill — no reason to walk all the way back down before you eat.

Bistecca alla Fiorentina

Florence's signature dish is a thick-cut T-bone, traditionally served very rare — this isn't the place to ask for medium-well. It's usually ordered by weight and meant to be shared, so it works well split between two people.

Open in Google Maps: Florentine Steak Restaurants

San Niccolò

This neighborhood sits right beneath Piazzale Michelangelo, which makes it the natural place to eat once you've come down from the view — restaurants and atmosphere without walking all the way back into the historic center.

Open in Google Maps: San Niccolò

Book Later Than You Think

Italians eat dinner later than a lot of American schedules assume — a reservation around 8:30–9 PM lines up with the local rhythm rather than an empty dining room.

A Few Things That Make the Day Easier

Small habits that keep you from feeling like a tourist by accident.

Cappuccino is technically a breakfast drink here, though no one will actually stop you from ordering one after noon. Espresso is usually taken standing at the bar — sitting down at a table, even for the same cup, often costs more. If a menu lists a coperto, that's a standard per-person cover charge, not a mistake on your bill. Tipping isn't expected at American percentages either; rounding up or leaving a few euros for good service is normal.

Getting Around Today

Your feet are the fastest way through this city, not a taxi.

The historic center is compact and largely pedestrianized, so walking should carry almost the entire day. Cobblestones and uneven paving are constant, so comfortable shoes matter more than they might in a flatter city. If you do need a bus or tram for something farther out, the current fare is €2 for 90 minutes of travel, with contactless payment accepted onboard.

If anyone in your group is arriving by taxi or a rental car, know that Florence's historic center has a camera-enforced restricted traffic zone (ZTL). Driving into it without authorization can mean a fine by mail — it only matters if a car is part of today's plans, but it catches people off guard.

Where Not to Waste Your One Day

None of this is worth the time it costs you today.

Avoid
Trying to fit the Uffizi, the Accademia, Pitti Palace, the Duomo climb, and Boboli into a single day. Each one deserves real time, and stacking all of them means you'll spend more of the day in ticket lines than in front of anything worth seeing.

Avoid
Eating your one meal directly beside the Duomo, Ponte Vecchio, or Piazza della Signoria. Walking even a few streets away consistently gets better food for the same money — and skip any restaurant with a giant photo menu and someone pulling pedestrians in off the street.

Avoid
Scheduling every single hour. Florence is a city where wandering down an interesting street, ducking into a small church, or stopping at a wine window is part of what the day is for — build in slack rather than filling it completely.

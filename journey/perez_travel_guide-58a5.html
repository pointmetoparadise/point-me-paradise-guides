1Before You Leave

Passports and Entry Rules

Switzerland, Germany and the Czech Republic are all part of Europe's Schengen Area, which works like one country for border purposes. You show your passport once when you enter the area, which on this trip happens in Amsterdam, not Zurich, and once when you leave, which happens in Paris on the way home. You will not pass through passport control at Zurich or Prague airports, and there are no border booths on the trains, although German police do sometimes walk through trains near the border and ask to see passports.

  • Passport validity: each passport must be valid for at least three months after November 29, so through at least March 1, 2027, and must have been issued within the last ten years. Two blank pages are recommended.

  • Visa: not required. U.S. citizens may visit the Schengen Area for up to 90 days in any 180-day period.

  • ETIAS: not required for this trip. ETIAS is a planned online authorization for U.S. visitors that is not yet operating. Do not pay any website that offers to process one.

  • Entry/Exit System (EES): Europe now records visitors digitally instead of stamping passports. When you arrive in Amsterdam, you will have your photo and fingerprints taken at a kiosk or booth. Nothing is needed in advance.

  • Return proof: officers may ask to see your return flight and your hotel details. Keep this guide or your itinerary handy on your phone.

  • Copies: take a photo of each passport's photo page and email it to yourselves, so a copy is available if a passport is lost.

Money Before You Go

CURRENCY NOTE

  • Call your bank and credit card companies to tell them your travel dates and countries: Netherlands, Switzerland, Germany, Czech Republic and France.

  • Bring at least two cards, ideally with no foreign transaction fees, and keep them in different places. Visa and Mastercard are the most widely accepted. American Express is accepted at your hotels but less often in shops and restaurants.

  • Know your debit card PIN. European machines ask for a four-digit PIN, and some ticket machines do not accept signature cards.

  • Set up Apple Pay or Google Pay on your phones. Tap-to-pay works almost everywhere on this route.

  • You do not need to buy foreign currency before leaving. Use a bank ATM after you arrive for a small amount of cash in each country.

Phones and Apps

  • Phone plan: either add your carrier's international day pass or buy a Europe eSIM before you leave. Switzerland is not in the European Union, so check that any plan you choose explicitly includes Switzerland as well as Germany and the Czech Republic.

  • WhatsApp: install it now. Your drivers in Zurich and Prague communicate by WhatsApp or text, and it is how most Europeans message.

  • Helpful apps: SBB Mobile (Swiss trains), DB Navigator (German trains and live platform numbers), PID Lítačka (Prague public transport tickets), Bolt or Uber (Prague taxis), and Google Translate with German and Czech downloaded for offline use.

  • Offline maps: in Google Maps, download Zurich, Munich and Prague so directions work without data.

Health and Insurance

  • No vaccinations are required for this trip.

  • U.S. health insurance generally does not work in Europe, so travel medical insurance is strongly recommended. Carry your policy number and emergency phone number.

  • Pack prescription medicines in your carry-on, in their original labeled containers, with enough for a few extra days.

Money in Prague

CURRENCY NOTEHONEST DRAWBACK

The Czech Republic uses the Czech koruna (CZK), not the euro. Some places accept euros, but at poor rates, so pay in koruna or by card. Cards are accepted almost everywhere, including many market stalls.

  • Use ATMs attached to a real bank, such as Česká spořitelna, ČSOB or Komerční banka. Avoid the standalone Euronet ATMs found on tourist streets, which charge high fees and push poor exchange rates.

  • Avoid street exchange booths advertising "0% commission". Their rates are often very poor.

  • If you need a taxi, use the Bolt or Uber app rather than hailing one on the street.

  • Check restaurant bills: some places near the square add a service charge or charge for bread placed on the table.

Money, Cards and Tipping

CURRENCY NOTE
Country Currency Suggested Cash Tipping
Switzerland Swiss franc (CHF) CHF 50 to 100 Service is included in prices. Round up, or leave 5 to 10% for excellent service.
Germany Euro (€) €100 to 150 About 5 to 10%. Tell the server the total you want to pay when you hand over your card, rather than leaving money on the table.
Czech Republic Czech koruna (CZK) CZK 2,000 to 3,000 About 10% in restaurants, unless a service charge is already on the bill.

The currency trap to avoid: when you pay by card or use an ATM, the machine will often ask whether you want to pay in U.S. dollars or the local currency. Always choose the local currency (CHF, EUR or CZK). Choosing dollars lets the machine's bank set its own exchange rate, which can cost you 5 to 10% more. This is called dynamic currency conversion, and it is the most common way travelers overpay in Europe.

Your private transfers in Zurich and Prague already include tips. Hotel housekeeping tips are not expected in Europe, though a few euros or francs for exceptional service is always appreciated.

Power and Charging

Europe uses 230 volts, compared with 120 in the U.S. Phone, tablet and laptop chargers handle both automatically (look for "100-240V" on the charger), but U.S. hair dryers and curling irons do not, so use the hotel's hair dryer instead.

  • Switzerland uses Type J outlets, recessed with three holes. Germany uses Type F. Czech Republic uses Type E.

  • A slim two-pin Type C "Europlug" adapter fits all three countries, including Swiss outlets. Bulky round German adapters often do not fit Swiss outlets, so bring at least two slim Type C adapters and a small U.S. power strip without surge protection.

Weather and Packing

Late November is cold and often damp, with typical highs around 41 to 43°F and lows around freezing in all three cities, and snow is possible, especially in the Alps on castle day. It gets dark around 4:30 PM, which is when the markets look their best.

  • A warm, water-resistant coat, plus layers you can add or remove, since shops, trains and restaurants are well heated.

  • A hat, gloves and a scarf. You will spend hours outdoors at the markets.

  • Comfortable waterproof shoes or boots with good grip. Old Town streets in all three cities are cobblestone, and they get slippery when wet or icy.

  • A smart casual outfit for the birthday dinner.

  • A small crossbody bag that zips closed, worn in front in crowds.

  • A compact umbrella and a refillable water bottle.

Luggage

You will handle your own bags on two trains and in and out of four cars, so pack lighter than you think you need. One medium suitcase each plus a carry-on is ideal. There are no luggage weight limits on European trains, but you must lift your bags onto racks yourselves, and Prague's cobblestones are hard on small suitcase wheels. Your flights include one checked bag each.

Time Zones

Switzerland, Germany and the Czech Republic are all 7 hours ahead of Houston. When it is 6:00 PM in Munich, it is 11:00 AM at home. All times in this guide are local times.

Health and Safety

HONEST DRAWBACK

All three cities are very safe, but crowded Christmas markets, trams and the Charles Bridge attract pickpockets. Keep phones out of back pockets, keep bags zipped and in front of you, and be wary of anyone who bumps into you or asks you to sign a petition. Glühwein is stronger than it tastes, and cobblestones are uneven, so go easy on both.

Pharmacies are marked with a green cross (Apotheke in Germany and Switzerland, Lékárna in Prague) and pharmacists usually speak English and can help with minor issues.

Everyday Customs for First-Time Visitors

  • Paying at restaurants: your server will not bring the bill until you ask for it, because rushing guests is considered rude. Catch their eye and say "Zahlen, bitte" (TSAH-len, BIT-teh) in Zurich and Munich.

  • Water is not free: restaurants serve bottled water and you will be asked "still or sparkling". Tap water is safe everywhere on this trip, but restaurants rarely bring it.

  • Public restrooms often cost money: usually CHF 1 to 2, €0.50 to 1, or CZK 10 to 20. Keep a few coins handy. Restaurants and cafés expect you to be a customer. In Prague, Ž means women and M means men. In Germany and Switzerland, look for Damen (women) and Herren (men).

  • Sundays are quiet: shops close on Sundays in Germany and most of Switzerland, though restaurants, museums and station shops stay open.

  • Bike lanes: in Munich and Zurich, red or marked bike lanes run alongside sidewalks. Do not walk in them, since cyclists move fast.

  • Crossing the street: wait for the green walking light, even when the street is empty. Locals take it seriously.

  • Reservations: book dinner ahead during market season, especially on weekends.

Tax-Free Shopping

VERIFY BEFORE TRAVEL

As visitors from outside Europe, you can reclaim part of the sales tax on larger purchases. Ask the shop for a tax-free form when you pay. Purchases from Germany and the Czech Republic are validated at Paris on your way home, before passport control, where you show the unused goods, receipts and passports. Swiss purchases must be validated before you leave Switzerland, which is awkward when traveling by train, so it is only worth it for a significant purchase. Minimum spends apply per shop, roughly CHF 300 in Switzerland, €50 in Germany and CZK 2,000 in the Czech Republic.

St. John's, Antigua: Your Port Day

A capital you can walk from the gangway, a Georgian dockyard to the south, and calm west-coast beaches ten minutes away.

You'll dock in the middle of town here. The cathedral's two white towers are often the first thing you pick out as the ship comes into the harbor, and the duty-free lanes of Heritage Quay start where the pier ends. Everything beyond town, from the Dockyard to the stingrays, sits a taxi ride or a short boat trip away. Most days still leave room for a swim.

Arriving at the Pier

Where your ship ties up decides how your morning starts.

Antigua Cruise Port has five berths. Most ships use the Heritage Quay Pier or the Nevis Street Pier (also called the Redcliffe Quay Pier), which sit almost side by side. Walk off either one and you're in a pedestrian shopping mall with boutiques, bars, a casino and, often, street performers. Tendering is rare here, but the port does offer it when needed.

Heritage Quay on Google Maps

If you land at Deep Water Harbour

On busy days, some ships go to Deep Water Harbour instead. It's an industrial port with no services, about 1.5 km from town and 10–15 minutes by taxi depending on traffic. It isn't a walk you'll want to make. Cruise Critic puts the taxi into town at around $10–12, and you should leave extra time to get back.

Deep Water Harbour on Google Maps

The night before: check your ship's berth assignment and the exact all-aboard time. Ships typically call from about 8am to 6pm, but your itinerary is what counts.

New construction at the waterfront

The port is finishing a new shopping, dining and leisure district beside the berths, expected to open this fall. A new terminal already opened in January 2026. The walk from pier to town may look different from older photos and reviews, so follow the port's signs and ask at the Guest Information Center if anything is unclear.

Getting Around

There are no meters and no rideshare apps here, so settle the fare before the door closes.

The port's authorized taxi provider is the St. John's Taxi Association. You'll find them through the Guest Information Center or the Taxi Dispatch Station. Drivers in orange shirts belong to the association and are the only ones allowed to approach you in the port area. Fares are regulated even without meters. Uber and Lyft don't operate in Antigua. The port also lists rentals, including cars, mopeds, scooters and bikes, in Zone 7.

Before you get in: ask whether the price is per person or per car, and whether it's in U.S. or Eastern Caribbean dollars. The beach fares in older forum posts ($5–8 per person each way) are likely low for today.

Expect a lot of selling as you come off the pier. Passengers often describe the wall of drivers and tour sellers as overwhelming the first time, and prices vary from one to the next. Know what you want before you walk out, and the pitch becomes easy to answer.

Getting a ride back

Many beach drivers will collect you at an agreed time and bring you back to the pier. Agree on the time, get the driver's name, and build in a cushion: be back at the pier an hour before all-aboard, or two hours if you're docked at Deep Water Harbour.

St. John's on Foot

Two hours, comfortable shoes, and a few Eastern Caribbean dollars cover most of the capital.

Heritage Quay & Redcliffe Quay

Heritage Quay is the duty-free strip right off the pier. Redcliffe Quay, just south, has more character, with old stone warehouses and shaded courtyards now filled with shops, restaurants and bars. Its buildings were once a slave compound, a history worth knowing as you walk through.

Redcliffe Quay on Google Maps

St. John's Cathedral

The cathedral of St. John the Divine sits on the rise above town, with its two 70-foot towers. It reopened for Easter 2018 after eight years of restoration, and restoration work continues. At the south gate, lead figures of the two St. Johns stand on the pillars; they're said to have been taken from a French ship in 1756. The iron gates date from 1789.

St. John's Cathedral on Google Maps

Good to know: this is a working church. Interior access depends on service times and restoration work, so be prepared to see it from the grounds.

Museum of Antigua and Barbuda

The museum is a few minutes' walk from the pier, in the old colonial courthouse. Inside you'll find a life-size replica of an Arawak house, exhibits on plantation life and slavery, and the cricket bat Viv Richards used in a test match against England. It's small, and that makes it easy to fit into a morning.

Museum of Antigua and Barbuda on Google Maps

Hours vary by source: typically 8:30am–4:30pm Monday–Saturday (one listing shows an earlier 3pm close on Fridays), and closed Sundays. One recent visitor paid about US$3. Bring small bills.

The Public Market

Market Street is where the island's produce comes to town. This is the place to look for the sweet Antigua black pineapple and to see what's actually growing here, away from the duty-free counters. Mornings are best, and weekends are reportedly the busiest.

St. John's Public Market on Google Maps

Fort James

A short taxi ride takes you to the fort at the harbor entrance, with wide views over the sea and back toward the city. Below it is Fort James Beach, the closest beach to the pier and a public stretch that's popular with locals.

Fort James on Google Maps

Rather have someone lead?

The port offers a two-hour St. John's City Walk that covers Heritage Quay, Redcliffe Quay, the Sir V.C. Bird bust and the Public Market. There's also a three-hour "Eat Like a Local" outing if you'd like to taste your way through town.

The Beaches

The closest sand is busy on ship days; the best ones are a little farther out.

Dickenson Bay

Dickenson Bay is about 3.8 km from St. John's and is the island's most developed beach, lined with hotels, restaurants and water-sports stands. Sandals Grande sits on it. You'll find chair and umbrella rentals, bathrooms, and food and drink without searching. The tradeoff is the crowd: on a multi-ship day, it fills up.

Dickenson Bay on Google Maps

Faster route: one visitor took a water taxi from the cruise port that stopped at Dickenson Bay or Runaway Beach (about $20 per person round trip at the time) and found it quicker than a car.

Runaway Bay

Runaway Bay sits just south of Dickenson Bay. It's narrower and less built up, with the same water and a calmer feel. If Dickenson looks packed when you arrive, this is where to go.

Runaway Bay on Google Maps

The southwest: Valley Church, Jolly, Darkwood

The southwest coast is less developed than the beaches around St. John's. Valley Church Beach is about 30 minutes by taxi. Jolly Beach is a mile of sand at Jolly Harbour, and Darkwood is about five minutes' drive south of it. These are your best options when several ships are in port.

Valley Church Beach on Google Maps Darkwood Beach on Google Maps

Pigeon Point

Near English Harbour, Pigeon Point is a local favorite with showers, restrooms, a playground and a beach bar. It's the natural place to cool off after a morning at Nelson's Dockyard.

Pigeon Point Beach on Google Maps

Reading the crowd

The port handled more than 800,000 passengers across 431 ship calls in 2025, so multi-ship days are normal. When you see several ships alongside, skip Dickenson Bay and head for Runaway Bay or the southwest. Bring water shoes and reef-safe sunscreen.

Nelson's Dockyard & Shirley Heights

The island's headline history sits in the south, and it's worth half your day.

Nelson's Dockyard

This Georgian-era naval yard at English Harbour is still a working dockyard, and it has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2016. Restored stone buildings line the water, and yachts tie up where Royal Navy ships once did. The park's own history is direct about who built it: more than three-quarters of the historic workforce were enslaved Africans, including shipwrights, blacksmiths, carpenters, caulkers and sailmakers. The Royal Navy closed the yard in 1889.

Nelson's Dockyard on Google Maps

Shirley Heights Lookout

Up on the ridge, the old signal station gives you the view of English Harbour and the Dockyard that ends up on most of the island's postcards. It was first used as a lookout during the American War of Independence. The ground is uneven, so wear flat shoes. It isn't wheelchair-accessible.

Shirley Heights Lookout on Google Maps

Dow's Hill Interpretation Center

The center's observation decks and exhibits provide context for the rest of the park. Many tours stop here between the Lookout and the Dockyard.

Dow's Hill Interpretation Center on Google Maps

Fees and hours

Visitors pay a one-time park fee, typically US$15, which covers the Dockyard, Shirley Heights, the Blockhouse and the Interpretation Center. One tour listing quotes US$23, so carry enough cash for either. The National Parks Authority lists hours of Monday–Saturday, 8am–5pm, and doesn't publish Sunday hours. One guide gives shorter daytime hours for Shirley Heights. If you're in port on a Sunday or Monday, call the National Parks Authority at (268) 481-5021 before you go.

Book a tour, not a ride: a passenger who priced a one-way taxi straight to the Dockyard found it cost far more than a round-island tour that included it.

Devil's Bridge & the East Coast

The Atlantic side is rougher, and the drive takes 30 to 40 minutes each way.

Devil's Bridge

Devil's Bridge is a natural limestone arch near Indian Town Point, east of Willikies, about 40 minutes from St. John's. Atlantic waves drive through it and spout from the blowholes around it. It has been a national park since 2008. The site also carries a painful history, linked to accounts of enslaved people who threw themselves into the sea here to escape.

Devil's Bridge on Google Maps

Look, don't cross: waves can wash completely over the arch and knock you into the water. There are no guard rails and no lifeguards. Stay well back from the edge and the blowholes.

Long Bay

Long Bay is fully sheltered by its reef, which keeps the water calm and makes it a good choice for families. It's the usual beach stop after Devil's Bridge.

Long Bay Beach on Google Maps

Half Moon Bay

Half Moon Bay is a crescent of sand on the far east coast, now a national park. It faces the Atlantic, so surf conditions change. It works best as part of a private tour rather than a separate round trip.

Half Moon Bay on Google Maps

Season check

September through November is the wettest stretch of the year and peak hurricane season. On a windy day the east coast gets rough. Itineraries can change, so have a town-and-beach plan in reserve.

On the Water

The rays come in close, and the catamarans take most of the day.

Stingray City

Stingray City is on a sandbar in Mercer Creek Bay, about 10 miles by road from town. You get a safety briefing in Seatons Village, take a ten-minute boat ride out, and stand in roughly four feet of water while southern stingrays glide around your legs. You don't need to know how to swim. There are changing rooms and restrooms back at the land base.

Price and timing: ship-run versions typically take about three hours, and one line lists it at around $95 per adult. Independent operators charge about $150 and meet cruise passengers outside the port. Take the earliest slot you can get, before the large groups arrive.

Catamaran sail & snorkel

Wadadli Cats runs day sails to Bird Island, to Cades Reef (a barrier reef that stretches about two miles), and around the whole island with lunch on Green Island. Boats board at Deep Water Harbour or Jolly Harbour Marina. The Cades Reef trip runs about six hours, and full circumnavigations by various operators run up to six and a half.

Watch the clock: six hours on the water is a lot of a ten-hour port day. Book these through your ship, or confirm a guaranteed return time in writing.

Licensed boats only

The port asks you to accept rides only from licensed operators, on land and at sea. That covers water taxis, jet skis, banana boats and speedboats as well as cars.

Tours: Ship-Run or Private

Here, a taxi driver often doubles as your guide.

Through your cruise line

Common ship-run options include Stingray City, Devil's Bridge with time on Long Bay (about four hours, including a drink and a beach chair), and history tours covering Shirley Heights, Dow's Hill and Nelson's Dockyard. The park fee is usually extra on history tours. Prices vary by sailing, so check yours.

Private and small-group

Antigua's taxi drivers are also qualified tour guides, and an island tour with a driver is a common way to spend the day. Passengers have described tours that cover the highlights, stop at a beach such as Valley Church, wait for them there, and bring them back when they're ready. For something different, a tuk-tuk tour (from about $129) covers the Dockyard, Shirley Heights, Galleon Beach and a local craft brewery, with park entry included.

Book through someone accountable: the port suggests booking through your cruise line or another established travel provider, and agreeing on the route, return time and total price before you leave the pier.

Who carries the timing risk

If a ship-run tour is late, the ship waits. If an independent tour is late, it may not. The farther you go, toward English Harbour or the east coast, the more that difference matters.

What to Eat & Bring Home

Order the national dish at least once.

Fungee and pepperpot is Antigua's national dish: a cornmeal and okra base served with a slow-cooked stew of meat and vegetables. Look also for ducana, a sweet-potato and coconut dumpling steamed in a banana leaf, and for johnnycakes (fried cornmeal dumplings), saltfish, and chop-up, a mash of eggplant, spinach and okra. Goatwater, a stew, often turns up at breakfast. There are plenty of places to eat around Heritage and Redcliffe Quays. Wash it down with a cold Wadadli, the local beer.

Worth packing

Susie's hot sauce, a bottle of local rum, island honey, or a piece of local pottery. All travel better than a black pineapple, though the pineapple is worth eating while you're here.

Planning Your Day

Choose one direction from the pier and leave the rest for next time.

Easy day. Spend the morning on the cathedral, the museum, the market and Redcliffe Quay. Then take a taxi to Runaway Bay or Fort James for the afternoon.
History day. Take a morning taxi tour to Nelson's Dockyard and Shirley Heights, swim at Pigeon Point, and be back in town by mid-afternoon.
Active day. Do the first Stingray City slot of the morning, then spend the afternoon on Dickenson Bay or Runaway Bay.
East-coast day. See Devil's Bridge and swim at Long Bay, through your ship or with a private driver who guarantees your return time.

Skip these on a port day

The Shirley Heights Sunday and Thursday sunset parties are evening events (Thursday runs 4:30–8:30pm), so they only work if your ship stays late or overnight. Day trips to Barbuda by ferry don't fit a standard port call. Don't walk out onto Devil's Bridge, and don't ride with unlicensed drivers or boat operators.

Before You Go Ashore

A few small things that keep the day running smoothly.

  • Money: the Eastern Caribbean dollar is the official currency, and U.S. dollars are widely accepted. Carry small U.S. bills plus some EC$ for the market and street food. Tipping tour guides is customary.
  • Park fee: bring cash for Nelson's Dockyard if you're going on your own.
  • Sundays: the museum is closed, and national park Sunday hours aren't published.
  • Local law: buying or using marijuana is illegal, with strict penalties.
  • Safety: the U.S. State Department rates Antigua and Barbuda Level 1, its lowest advisory level, and describes it as generally safe. Normal caution applies, and quiet side streets after dark are the main thing to avoid.
  • Gear: flat shoes for Shirley Heights, water shoes and reef-safe sunscreen for the beaches, and plenty of water.

The one rule that matters

Be back at the pier an hour before all-aboard, or two hours if you're docked at Deep Water Harbour. Everything else on the island can be adjusted around that.

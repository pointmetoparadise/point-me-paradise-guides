St. Moritz & The Bernina Express

A day of glacier light, red trains, and a lake that changes color with the hour.

What to Expect

The glamour is real, but it's not the point

You came for the scenery — St. Moritz just happens to wear a nice coat.

St. Moritz sits at about 1,800 meters (5,900 feet) in Switzerland's Upper Engadin valley, in the canton of Graubünden. The town's reputation for grand hotels, designer boutiques and champagne terraces is well earned, but the surrounding Engadin is really about something else entirely: lakes, glaciers, mountain railways and small Alpine villages. That contrast is worth knowing going in, because it means you don't have to be a ski-resort person to have a genuinely good day here.

The town itself splits into a few distinct pockets. St. Moritz Dorf is the hillside center — hotels, boutiques, restaurants, the walkable core. St. Moritz Bad sits lower, down by the lake, with more of the spa-and-sports feel. For a shorter visit built around the lake, the rail journey and a bit of wandering, Dorf is the easier base — everything worth reaching on foot is close together.

Getting around

Trains and buses connect everything in this valley — St. Moritz, Pontresina, Celerina, the mountain stations. You genuinely don't need a car here, and for a day built around the railway, you won't want one.

Weather & What to Wear

Dress for altitude, not for the season back home

Layers, sturdy shoes, and sunglasses you actually trust.

At close to 1,800 meters, and with parts of the rail route climbing above 3,000 meters, conditions here run cooler and change faster than they would at sea level — that's just how mountain weather behaves, regardless of season. Bring layers you can add or shed through the day, a jacket that blocks wind, and shoes comfortable enough for walking on uneven ground, cobblestones, and platform transfers.

Sun at altitude is stronger than it feels. Sunglasses and sunscreen are worth packing even if the day looks mild — the glare off snow, glaciers, or the lake surface adds up quickly. Check the forecast close to your travel date rather than relying on general seasonal expectations; alpine weather can shift within a single day, and that's part of why it's worth staying a little flexible about which mountain viewpoint or outdoor stop you save for the clearest part of the day.

Passport Reminder

You're crossing into Italy — bring the right document

One small detail that's easy to forget on a day that otherwise feels entirely Swiss.

The Bernina rail route crosses the border between Switzerland and Italy, ending in Tirano. Carry your passport or appropriate travel documentation with you for the day — don't leave it back at the hotel. It's also worth knowing that currency changes at the border: Switzerland uses Swiss francs (CHF), while Tirano uses euros. Cards are widely accepted on both sides, but a small amount of CHF is useful for incidentals in Switzerland.

The Scenery

The railway is the main event

Forty-two tunnels, a hundred-plus bridges, and views that don't let up.

The Albula/Bernina railway has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2008, and it earns that status through sheer engineering — the Bernina section alone runs 61 kilometers with 13 tunnels and 52 viaducts and bridges. The classic run from St. Moritz to Tirano takes roughly 2 hours 15 minutes on the branded Bernina Express, climbing to 2,253 meters at Ospizio Bernina before descending all the way to 429 meters at Tirano.

Along the way, keep an eye out for Lago Bianco — a milky turquoise lake right beside the tracks at the highest point of the route, with glaciers and bare high-alpine terrain on all sides. Alp Grüm offers views toward the Palü Glacier and Val Poschiavo. And just before Tirano, the train loops around itself on the Brusio Spiral Viaduct, a stone spiral built to lose elevation without a rack railway — one of the more memorable few minutes of the whole trip.

Bernina Express or regional train?

You don't have to book the branded Bernina Express to see this route — regional trains run the same tracks, more frequently, with the freedom to hop off along the way. The Express gives you reserved seating and large panoramic windows, which makes it the easier, more relaxed choice for a group that wants the classic experience without managing stops. Rhaetian Railway recommends seat reservations on busy regional trains too, and those currently run about CHF 5.

Pack light for the train — luggage-rack space is limited, and you'll be glad to have your hands free for photos.

Food & Snacks

Coffee, nut cake, and a picnic that beats the price tag

The local specialty is a cake, not a fondue pot.

Start with Kaffeehaus Hanselmann, a St. Moritz institution dating to the late 19th century — the draw is coffee, pastries, chocolates and especially the Engadine nut cake (Nusstorte), which is the regional specialty worth seeking out over generic Swiss chocolate. Hauser, more central and a touch more casual, is a good second stop for bakery items and its own well-regarded Nusstorte — either makes a good food souvenir to bring home.

Keeping costs sane

St. Moritz can get expensive fast. A picnic of bread, Bündnerfleisch (air-dried beef), cheese and a slice of Nusstorte, eaten by the lake, is an easy way to keep the day affordable without giving anything up — supermarkets and bakeries are the simplest route to good food at a normal price.

Free Time in St. Moritz

What to do with an open hour or two

The lake, a leaning tower, and a street that's fun to window-shop even if you buy nothing.

Lake St. Moritz is the easiest way to spend downtime well — a walk along the shore costs nothing and gives you the full setting: green mountains, the town rising up the hillside, and the water itself, which shifts color through the day. If you'd rather browse, Via Serlas is the town's famous luxury shopping street, lined with international designer boutiques — worth a walk even if you're not buying, since it's part of the St. Moritz experience.

For something quieter, the Segantini Museum holds the world's most comprehensive collection of Giovanni Segantini's paintings, and the Engadin landscape was central to his work — a nice fit if anyone in your group leans toward art over shopping. In central Dorf, the Leaning Tower — the surviving 12th-century tower of St. Mauritius Church, 33 meters tall and leaning about 5.5 degrees — is an easy few-minutes stop while you're already out walking, not something that needs its own trip.

If your schedule allows extra time beyond the town itself, Muottas Muragl is worth knowing about: a historic funicular (running since 1905) climbs to 2,456 meters for a sweeping view down the chain of Upper Engadin lakes, with a restaurant at the top. Late afternoon tends to be the better time to go, when the light comes in low across the lakes.

A Few Things to Know

Small details that make the day easier

None of these are dealbreakers — just worth knowing before you go.

Timing on the rail line

The Bernina line tends to be less busy Monday through Thursday. On weekends, morning trains heading north and afternoon trains heading south tend to fill up first — worth factoring in if you have flexibility on which day you ride.

Not every shop, restaurant, or mountain lift runs year-round here — seasonal closures are real, so it's worth checking that anything specific you're hoping to see or do is actually open on the date you're traveling. And if the day feels like it's filling up fast, it's fine to leave some of it unplanned — mountain weather changes quickly, and having room to move a viewpoint or a stop to the clearest window of the day is usually worth more than a packed schedule.
